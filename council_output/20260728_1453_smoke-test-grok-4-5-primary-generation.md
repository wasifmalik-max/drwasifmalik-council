## Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) accounts for approximately 85% of all strokes and remains one of the leading causes of death and long-term disability worldwide. Globally, nearly 12 million new stroke cases occur each year, with a lifetime risk approaching 25%. In-hospital mortality ranges from 10–15%, and without timely reperfusion, permanent disability is common.  

In Pakistan the burden is substantial: crude prevalence estimates range from 1.2% to 4.8%, with an annual incidence near 250 per 100,000 population. Hypertension and diabetes drive roughly 70% of cases, and patients frequently present at younger ages than in Western cohorts. Limited dedicated stroke units outside major cities compound delays. At Faraz Hospital, Bahawalpur, we see these realities daily and focus on rapid recognition, evidence-based reperfusion, and secondary prevention to change outcomes.

## What is it?

Acute ischemic stroke occurs when a cerebral artery is occluded by thrombus or embolus, producing abrupt focal brain ischemia. Neurons begin to die within minutes in the core infarct; the surrounding penumbra remains potentially salvageable if blood flow is restored quickly.  

AIS is distinguished from hemorrhagic stroke by the absence of vessel rupture. Large-vessel occlusion (LVO) involving the internal carotid or proximal middle cerebral artery carries the highest risk of malignant edema and poor outcome if untreated. Smaller perforator occlusions produce lacunar syndromes. Time is brain: every minute of untreated LVO destroys approximately 1.9 million neurons. Modern care therefore prioritizes door-to-needle and door-to-puncture times, supported by advanced imaging that identifies salvageable tissue even beyond traditional windows. Understanding this pathophysiology underpins every decision we make in the neurosurgical and stroke pathway at Faraz Hospital.

## Causes

The dominant etiologies are atherosclerosis of large arteries, cardioembolism (commonly atrial fibrillation), and small-vessel lipohyalinosis. In Pakistan, uncontrolled hypertension and diabetes mellitus together account for the majority of cases. Additional risk factors include dyslipidemia, smoking, obesity, sedentary lifestyle, and prior transient ischemic attack.  

Prothrombotic states, cervical artery dissection, and rare vasculopathies appear more often in younger patients. Delayed presentation is frequent because of low public awareness and transport barriers, converting potentially reversible ischemia into established infarction. Addressing modifiable vascular risks through aggressive blood-pressure control, glycemic management, and lifestyle change remains the most effective population-level strategy.

## Symptoms - bold red flags

Recognizing stroke symptoms instantly is critical. Use the FAST framework and watch for these **red flags**:

- **Face drooping** – sudden unilateral facial weakness or asymmetry  
- **Arm weakness** – inability to raise one arm or sudden drift  
- **Speech difficulty** – slurred speech, word-finding problems, or inability to speak  
- **Time to call emergency services** – note the exact time of onset  

Additional urgent signs include sudden **vision loss** in one or both eyes, **severe thunderclap headache**, **dizziness or loss of balance**, **numbness** on one side of the body, and **confusion** or altered consciousness. Any of these symptoms, even if fleeting, warrant immediate medical evaluation. Do not wait for symptoms to improve; penumbral tissue is lost with every passing minute. Family members should bring medication lists and note the last known well time—this single piece of information determines eligibility for thrombolysis or thrombectomy.

## Treatment

Treatment rests on rapid reperfusion, supportive care, and prevention of complications. Intravenous thrombolysis with alteplase is recommended within 4.5 hours of onset for eligible patients, a practice supported by foundational evidence and endorsed by AAN and NICE guidelines.

## Recovery

Recovery begins within 24 hours and continues for months. Early mobilization, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and speech-language therapy maximize neuroplasticity. Blood-pressure and glycemic control, high-intensity statins, and antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy (according to etiology) reduce recurrence risk.  

Many patients regain substantial function with structured rehabilitation; however, residual deficits in strength, language, or cognition are common without timely reperfusion. Family education on home exercises, medication adherence, and recognition of recurrent symptoms is essential. Follow-up imaging and clinic reviews at Faraz Hospital track progress and adjust secondary prevention. Psychological support addresses post-stroke depression, which affects a significant proportion of survivors.

## FAQ

**1. How long do I have to receive stroke treatment?**  
Intravenous thrombolysis is most effective within 4.5 hours; mechanical thrombectomy can help selected patients up to 24 hours when imaging shows salvageable tissue.

**2. What makes Faraz Hospital different for stroke care?**  
We offer rapid BodyTom CT imaging, Zeiss microscope-assisted neurosurgery, guideline-based reperfusion pathways, and direct access to a consultant neurosurgeon experienced in both medical and surgical stroke management.

**3. Can young people have ischemic strokes?**  
Yes. In Pakistan, younger onset is relatively common due to uncontrolled hypertension, diabetes, and other vascular risks.

**4. Is every stroke patient a candidate for thrombectomy?**  
No. Thrombectomy is reserved for large-vessel occlusions with appropriate clinical and imaging criteria. Many patients benefit from thrombolysis or best medical therapy alone.

**5. How can I reduce my risk of a first or recurrent stroke?**  
Control blood pressure and diabetes, stop smoking, take prescribed antiplatelets or anticoagulants, lower cholesterol, exercise regularly, and seek immediate care for any FAST symptoms.
