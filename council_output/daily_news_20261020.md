# Recurrent GBM Reoperation Criteria: Selecting Surgery vs Medical Care

Recurrent glioblastoma (GBM) forces a hard choice between another operation, systemic therapy, supportive care, or a clinical trial. Re-resection is not automatic. Benefit is most plausible when performance status is preserved, the target is focal and safely accessible, and surgery can meaningfully reduce mass effect without trading months of independence for new deficit. This brief summarizes practical clinical and radiographic factors used to favor reoperation versus non-surgical paths.

## What changed / Why it matters

Modern selection still rests on morbidity risk, functional reserve, and realistic survival gain rather than imaging alone. Factors that commonly **favor re-resection** include: Karnofsky Performance Status typically ≥70 (or ECOG 0–1); a relatively longer progression-free interval after first-line treatment; unilateral, discrete enhancing recurrence rather than multifocal or gliomatosis-like spread; location outside or only marginally involving eloquent cortex/white-matter pathways where a safe corridor and achievable cytoreduction exist; symptomatic mass effect, steroid dependence, or hydrocephalus that debulking can relieve; and patient goals aligned with an aggressive local strategy after informed discussion of wound, infection, and neurologic risk—especially after prior radiation.

Factors that more often **favor medical therapy, best supportive care, or trial enrollment** include: declining performance status or rapid functional loss; diffuse, multifocal, or deep midline/brainstem-predominant disease; recurrence largely inseparable from critical language/motor networks where expected residual would remain large; short interval to true progression with poor prior tolerance of treatment; significant medical comorbidity or healing risk (e.g., bevacizumab-era tissue concerns, scalp compromise); and situations where imaging suggests treatment effect versus tumor but clinical decline is driven by non-surgical drivers. Radiographic review should integrate prior radiation fields, perfusion/advanced MRI when available, and pattern of enhancement, yet no single scan replaces bedside function and surgical anatomy.

Evidence synthesis across eras shows that when reoperation helps, it is usually in selected patients who undergo substantial safe debulking and remain well enough for subsequent systemic or trial therapy. Median survival increments are measured in months for carefully chosen cases, not guaranteed long-term control; unselected reoperation can add deficit without clear survival upside. Molecular context (e.g., MGMT methylation) and eligibility for trials may tilt timing—sometimes biopsy or limited resection is enough to confirm pathology and open trial doors without maximalist second surgery.

## Patient takeaway

Reoperation for recurrent GBM is a selective tool: best considered for fit patients with focal, accessible disease and symptoms tied to mass effect; less appropriate when function is already poor or disease is diffuse. Ask your team how much tumor can be removed safely, what deficit risk is realistic, how steroids and rehab factor in, and whether a trial or medical regimen better matches your goals. Second surgery is one option in a continuum, not a default.
