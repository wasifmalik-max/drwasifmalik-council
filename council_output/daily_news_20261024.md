# Hemangioblastoma and VHL Counseling: Timing Resection and Lifelong Multi-Organ Screening

Cerebellar or spinal hemangioblastoma is rarely “just a brain tumor.” In a substantial share of patients it is the neurosurgical presentation of von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) disease—an inherited multi-organ predisposition that continues long after the craniotomy or laminectomy is closed. Counseling at the time of diagnosis therefore has to join operative decision-making with a durable surveillance plan, not end at the discharge summary.

## What changed / Why it matters
Modern practice still centers on **symptomatic** lesions: progressive ataxia, hydrocephalus, brainstem compression, or myelopathy generally favor timely microsurgical resection when anatomy and patient fitness allow, because decompression and tissue diagnosis both matter and because untreated mass effect can produce irreversible deficit. **Asymptomatic** small lesions are often observed with serial imaging rather than reflexively excised, especially when multiplicity or deep eloquence raises the cost of surgery. After resection, **residual or intentionally left tumor**—common when the nodule sits on critical pia or when cystic walls are not the true neoplastic driver—needs structured MRI follow-up; growth, new cysts, or new symptoms—not the mere presence of a thin remnant—usually drive re-intervention discussion. Radiation or systemic options enter selected, carefully framed scenarios (recurrent, unresectable, or high surgical risk disease) and do not replace the need for genetic and systemic work-up.

The decisive practice link is **VHL systemic screening obligations**. A CNS hemangioblastoma should trigger consideration of germline evaluation and, when VHL is confirmed or strongly suspected, coordinated lifelong surveillance: neuraxis MRI, ophthalmologic review for retinal angiomas, abdominal imaging for renal cell carcinoma and pancreatic lesions, and assessment for pheochromocytoma and other VHL-spectrum tumors. Neurosurgery that ignores this pathway treats only the index mass and leaves preventable second primaries and metachronous CNS disease unmonitored. Equally, screening protocols must be individualized for age, prior resections, residual disease, and pregnancy or comorbidity—not applied as a rigid checklist without clinical judgment.

## Patient takeaway
If you or a family member has a hemangioblastoma, ask whether VHL testing and a written multi-organ surveillance calendar have been arranged, who owns the follow-up (neurosurgery, genetics, oncology, ophthalmology, urology), and how residual tumor on MRI will be watched. Report new headache, balance change, visual symptoms, blood-pressure spikes, or abdominal pain promptly. Surgery addresses the lesion that is hurting you now; VHL counseling protects the decades after.
