# Acromegaly surgical remission markers

Early biochemical checkpoints after resection of a growth hormone (GH)–secreting pituitary adenoma remain the practical bridge between operative success and lasting cardiometabolic risk reduction. Remission is not declared by imaging alone; it rests on timed GH and IGF-1 thresholds that decide whether observation is safe or whether medical therapy or stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) should be started without delay.

## What changed / Why it matters
Contemporary endocrine–neurosurgical consensus still anchors surgical remission in two complementary signals. In the immediate postoperative window (often day 1–7, and again at 6–12 weeks once glucocorticoids and fluid shifts settle), a random GH below approximately 1 µg/L—or a nadir GH below ~0.4 µg/L during oral glucose tolerance testing when that test is used—predicts durable control with high specificity. IGF-1 must subsequently normalize for age and sex; discordant pairs (low GH with still-elevated IGF-1, or the reverse) are common and warrant repeat paired assays rather than premature escalation.  

When these markers remain above threshold, guidelines support prompt addition of a somatostatin receptor ligand, a dopamine agonist, or GH-receptor antagonism, with SRS reserved for residual tumor that is surgically inaccessible or medically refractory. The clinical stakes are cardiometabolic: uncontrolled GH/IGF-1 excess drives hypertension, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and cardiomyopathy. Documented biochemical remission after surgery is associated with measurable improvement in these risk factors, which is why the same laboratory cut-offs that define “cure” also function as triggers to intensify adjuvant care before end-organ damage accumulates. Serial IGF-1, not a single postoperative MRI, therefore guides the timeline for medical therapy or SRS referral in most multidisciplinary pathways.

## Patient takeaway
After surgery for acromegaly, expect scheduled GH and IGF-1 blood tests in the first days and again over the following months. Normal results usually mean watchful follow-up; persistently high values mean your team will discuss medication or focused radiation to protect the heart, blood pressure, and metabolism—not a failure of surgery, but the planned next step.
