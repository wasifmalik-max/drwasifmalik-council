# Meningioma Simpson grade in the MRI era

Meningioma surgery still leans on a decades-old idea: how completely the tumour and its dural attachment are removed shapes the chance it returns. The Simpson grade (I–V) was built for that purpose in an era of limited imaging. Today, high-resolution MRI, standardised residual-tumour reporting, WHO grade, and emerging histomolecular risk features sit alongside operative notes. The practical question for clinic and follow-up is whether Simpson grade still sets residual-tumour risk and surveillance intervals when modern MRI is the yardstick of “complete” resection.

## What changed / Why it matters

Classically, lower Simpson grades (especially I–II: gross total resection with or without dural/sinus management as described) were linked to lower recurrence than subtotal removal (III–V). Contemporary series and guideline-oriented reviews continue to show that extent of resection remains a major driver of local control, but MRI has refined what “residual” means. Thin-slice post-contrast MRI soon after surgery can show thin dural enhancement, unexpected nodules, or sinus involvement that the operative grade alone may under- or over-call. Thus Simpson grade is best read together with early postoperative MRI: a declared Simpson I/II with clear residual on MRI should be managed as residual disease for counselling and imaging plans; a higher Simpson grade with no measurable enhancing disease still warrants closer watch than a true radiographic gross total resection.

Histomolecular stratification adds a second axis. WHO grade 2–3 tumours, high mitotic activity, brain invasion, and unfavourable molecular profiles raise recurrence risk even after aggressive resection. Conversely, many grade 1 meningiomas after radiographic gross total resection have low short- to mid-term recurrence rates, so surveillance can be spaced more rationally rather than by operative label alone. Practice relevance is concrete: document Simpson grade in the operative note, obtain baseline MRI (timing per institutional protocol, often within the early postoperative window when surgically appropriate), measure any residual, then set interval MRI by the combination of residual volume, WHO/molecular risk, location (skull base, parasagittal/sinus), and symptoms—not by grade number in isolation. Patients with measurable residual or higher-risk histology generally need tighter early surveillance; low-risk, MRI-clear grade 1 disease may follow longer intervals after an initial stable scan, always individualised.

## Patient takeaway

Simpson grade still matters as a shared language for how much tumour and dura were removed, but MRI and tumour biology decide residual-tumour risk and how often scans are needed. Ask for your resection grade, your first postoperative MRI result (any measurable leftover tissue), and a written follow-up schedule matched to those findings.
