# Insular glioma approach corridors

Insular gliomas occupy a high-risk eloquent zone bounded by middle cerebral artery branches, lenticulostriate perforators, and critical language and sensorimotor networks. Safe resection therefore depends less on a single “best” corridor and more on disciplined case selection, corridor choice matched to tumor epicenter and laterality, and rigorous functional mapping so that extent of resection is balanced against permanent neurological cost.

## What changed / Why it matters
Two principal surgical corridors remain central in contemporary practice. The **transsylvian** route opens the sylvian fissure to reach the insular surface with relatively limited cortical transgression; it favors lesions with a dominant insular component and careful microdissection around M2–M3 branches and perforators, but demands meticulous vascular control and can be constrained by a narrow working angle or a swollen operculum. The **transcortical (transopercular)** corridor crosses frontal and/or temporal opercula—often after subpial resection of non-eloquent corridors identified by mapping—and can improve angles to superior or inferior insular zones and deeper extensions toward the extreme/external capsule. Choice is individualized by MRI anatomy, dominance, and whether the tumor is purely insular or operculo-insular.

Functional adjuncts are now standard rather than optional in eligible patients. Awake craniotomy with direct electrical stimulation, complemented by preoperative functional MRI, diffusion tractography, and when available navigated transcranial magnetic stimulation, helps define language, motor, and sometimes cognitive boundaries before and during resection. Continuous monitoring and subcortical stimulation reduce the chance of injuring the corticospinal tract, arcuate and superior longitudinal fasciculi, and uncinate pathways that frame the insula. Morbidity data across modern series consistently show that temporary deficits (especially speech or mild motor change) are relatively common after aggressive but mapped resection, whereas permanent disabling deficits are lower when surgeons stop at positive stimulation sites, respect perforator anatomy, and avoid forced resection beyond safe functional and vascular limits. Ischemic injury from lenticulostriate compromise remains a principal source of lasting harm and is a stronger driver of poor outcome than pure cortical entry when mapping is used appropriately. High-quality practice therefore prioritizes realistic goals—maximal safe resection or staged strategies—over heroic removal in unfavorable anatomy.

## Patient takeaway
If you or a family member faces an insular glioma discussion, ask how the team will choose between fissure-based and cortex-based access, whether awake mapping is planned, how vascular and white-matter risk will be monitored, and what neurological changes are expected to be temporary versus lasting. Outcomes hinge on matching the corridor and mapping plan to your tumor and brain organization, not on a one-size approach.
