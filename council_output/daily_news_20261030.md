# Dural-based mets mimicking meningioma

Dural-based masses are often labeled “meningioma” on first MRI review, yet a subset are metastases that share location, enhancement, and even a dural tail. Treating every extra-axial enhancing lesion as benign meningioma risks wrong histology assumptions, delayed systemic staging, and adjuvant plans that do not match the true disease.

## What changed / Why it matters
Modern multiparametric MRI and disciplined surgical decision-making have sharpened the separation between meningioma and dural metastasis without requiring novel trial IDs. Imaging red flags that should interrupt an automatic meningioma pathway include known solid-tumor history (breast, lung, prostate, renal, melanoma), rapid interval growth, irregular or lobulated margins, marked peritumoral edema disproportionate to lesion size, calvarial destruction or permeative bone change rather than hyperostosis, heterogeneous or ring-like enhancement, and diffusion or perfusion patterns more aggressive than typical WHO grade 1 meningioma. A classic dural tail does not exclude metastasis. When these features cluster—or when there is no long stable imaging archive—tissue diagnosis takes priority over empiric observation or meningioma-style radiosurgery alone.

Biopsy versus resection hinges on goals and anatomy. For accessible convexity lesions with mass effect, neurologic deficit, or diagnostic uncertainty, microsurgical resection achieves decompression, secure hemostasis, and adequate tumor for histology and molecular markers, while allowing inspection of the pial–dural interface. Deep, eloquent, or widely en plaque disease may start with targeted biopsy (open or image-guided) plus staging CT/PET as indicated, especially if a systemic primary is already active. Frozen section can redirect intraoperative strategy, but final paraffin diagnosis governs adjuvant therapy: meningioma pathways (observation, fractionated radiotherapy, or SRS by grade and residual) differ fundamentally from metastasis pathways (cavity SRS or fractionated RT, systemic therapy, and primary-site control). Assuming meningioma histology from imaging alone can produce inappropriate adjuvant plans—either undertreating metastatic disease or over-irradiating a lesion that needed systemic focus first.

Practice point for the clinic: document red-flag language in the MRI report discussion, avoid locking the consent to “meningioma resection” when mimicry is plausible, and align oncology early when metastasis ranks high on the differential. This sequence prevents incorrect histologic assumptions and mismatched adjuvant intent.

## Patient takeaway
Not every dural “meningioma” on MRI is benign. If you have a cancer history, fast-growing extra-axial tumor, heavy swelling, or bone erosion, ask whether biopsy or resection is needed before radiotherapy or watchful waiting. Correct tissue diagnosis guides whether treatment stays neurosurgical–radiotherapeutic for meningioma or becomes coordinated cancer care for metastasis. Bring prior scans and oncology records to consultation so timing of surgery versus staging is clear.
