# Second-look surgery after neoadjuvant therapy

In selected brain tumours, the traditional sequence of maximal safe resection first is being reconsidered. When molecular features predict meaningful response to systemic or targeted agents, a planned course of neoadjuvant therapy followed by second-look resection is emerging as a rational strategy to shrink tumour burden, clarify residual disease, and improve the safety and completeness of the definitive operation.

## What changed / Why it matters
Historically, neurosurgeons aimed for earliest possible cytoreduction because most primary brain tumours were viewed as poorly responsive to preoperative drugs and because mass effect often demanded prompt decompression. Advances in molecular profiling have altered that calculus for defined subgroups. Tumours harbouring targetable alterations (for example certain kinase fusions, IDH-related metabolic vulnerabilities, or hormone-receptor pathways in selected meningiomas and metastases) can show measurable volumetric reduction or metabolic quieting after a finite neoadjuvant window. When imaging and clinical status remain stable, a delayed second-look procedure can then address a smaller, better-demarcated residual, potentially lowering risk to eloquent cortex and white-matter tracts while still achieving the oncologic goal of maximal safe resection.

The practical shift is one of timing and selection, not abandonment of surgery. Multidisciplinary teams now integrate next-generation sequencing or immunohistochemistry early, discuss neoadjuvant options with medical oncology, and schedule interval imaging to decide whether the planned re-resection window remains appropriate. Operative planning benefits from updated functional MRI or tractography after therapy-induced changes. Pathology from the second look also supplies fresh tissue to confirm target engagement or emergent resistance, informing adjuvant choices. This paradigm is most relevant for molecularly enriched, non-acutely herniating patients; it does not replace emergency decompression when raised intracranial pressure or progressive deficit demands immediate action.

For the operating surgeon the key questions become: Is the molecular target credible? Is the expected response durable enough to justify delay? And can second-look surgery still be performed at a centre equipped for advanced monitoring and intraoperative adjuncts? When answers align, operative timing may legitimately move later in the pathway for carefully chosen cases.

## Patient takeaway
If your tumour carries a clear molecular marker, your team may recommend a short course of targeted or systemic therapy before a planned second operation. This is not “delay for delay’s sake”; it is an attempt to make surgery safer and more complete. Ask whether your case fits this approach, what imaging will be used to judge response, and how the surgical plan will be updated. Acute symptoms or rising pressure still require prompt surgical attention regardless of molecular status.
