# Molecular neuropathology for the operating surgeon

For the operating neurosurgeon, the 2021 WHO Classification of CNS Tumours (WHO CNS5) is not an abstract taxonomy exercise—it dictates what tissue must be preserved in the theatre, which molecular tests must be ordered on the request form, and how adjuvant radiotherapy and systemic therapy are sequenced after glioma or meningioma resection. Getting this pathway right starts before the specimen leaves the scrub nurse’s table.

## What changed / Why it matters

WHO CNS5 embeds molecular definition into diagnosis for diffuse gliomas and elevates selected molecular findings in meningioma risk stratification. Adult-type diffuse gliomas are stratified first by IDH1/IDH2 status. IDH-mutant tumours are further refined by 1p/19q codeletion (oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted) versus ATRX/TP53 patterns typical of IDH-mutant astrocytoma. IDH-wildtype diffuse gliomas meeting molecular glioblastoma criteria (for example TERT promoter mutation, EGFR amplification, or +7/−10 copy-number changes) are classified and treated as glioblastoma even when classic microvascular proliferation or necrosis is absent on the slides. CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion upgrades IDH-mutant astrocytoma grade and influences intensity of adjuvant planning. MGMT promoter methylation remains a key predictive marker for alkylating chemotherapy benefit and should be requested whenever temozolomide or PCV-type regimens are contemplated.

For meningiomas, histology still anchors grading, but TERT promoter mutation and CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion are associated with more aggressive behaviour and help identify patients who may need closer imaging surveillance or earlier consideration of adjuvant radiotherapy after subtotal resection or atypical/anaplastic histology. Integrated reports therefore combine morphology, immunohistochemistry, and a focused molecular panel rather than morphology alone.

Practical theatre implications are straightforward. Submit adequate viable tumour—avoid pure necrotic core or heavily cauterised margins—and ensure the pathologist receives a clear clinical note (prior radiation, known cancer, eloquent location). Where institutional pathways allow, reserve material suitable for DNA-based assays (and, when relevant, methylation profiling referral). On the postoperative request, explicitly list IDH, 1p/19q, and the glioma or meningioma markers your multidisciplinary team uses for adjuvant decisions; do not assume “routine histology” will trigger the full panel. Multidisciplinary discussion should wait for the integrated diagnosis before locking radiotherapy fields, dose, and chemotherapy choice, and counselling should reflect molecularly defined prognosis and treatment sensitivity rather than older purely histologic labels.

## Patient takeaway

After brain tumour surgery, the laboratory tests on the removed tissue often decide whether chemotherapy will help, how closely scans must be repeated, and whether extra radiation is advised. Ask your surgeon and oncologist for the integrated (histology plus molecular) result and how it changes your personal plan—especially IDH and related markers in gliomas, and higher-risk molecular findings in selected meningiomas.
