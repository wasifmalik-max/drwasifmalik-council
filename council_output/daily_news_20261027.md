# Seizure control after glioma resection

For many adults with diffuse glioma, the first clinic conversation is not only about survival curves or MRI timelines—it is about whether the next seizure will interrupt work, driving, or family life. Seizure freedom after resection is frequently ranked by patients as equal in importance to traditional oncologic endpoints. Contemporary glioma practice therefore treats perioperative antiepileptic drug (AED) decisions and the oncologic extent of resection as linked levers that shape both tumour control and epilepsy outcome.

## What changed / Why it matters

Evidence syntheses and guideline-aligned series over recent years have reinforced two practical points. First, in patients who present with seizures, a strategy aimed at safe maximal resection—when anatomy and functional mapping allow—is associated with higher rates of postoperative seizure control than biopsy or deliberately limited debulking alone. Greater volumetric reduction of epileptogenic tumour and peritumoural cortex reduces the substrate that drives focal seizures; residual contrast-enhancing or FLAIR-defined disease is more often linked to persistent or recurrent epilepsy. Second, perioperative AED policy has become more selective. Routine long-term prophylaxis in truly seizure-naïve patients is generally not mandated by major societies once the immediate perioperative window has passed, whereas patients with preoperative epilepsy usually continue an appropriate AED through surgery and early recovery, with later weaning guided by seizure-free interval, pathology, residual disease, and EEG when indicated. Enzyme-inducing older agents are increasingly replaced by better-tolerated options that interact less with chemotherapy and steroids, but the core principle remains individualised risk rather than automatic lifelong polytherapy. Mapping, awake protocols when language or sensorimotor cortex is at risk, and careful haemostasis still matter: new neurologic deficits and postoperative complications can themselves provoke seizures and offset the gain from a more complete resection. For low-grade and selected high-grade tumours, discussing “seizure outcome” beside progression-free survival helps align the operation with what patients actually value day to day.

## Patient takeaway

If you have a glioma and seizures, ask how much of the lesion can be removed safely and what the plan is for your AED before and after surgery. Seizure freedom is a realistic goal for many when resection is maximised and medicines are adjusted thoughtfully—not stopped abruptly. Report any aura, breakthrough event, or drug side effect early so the regimen can be refined while oncology treatment continues.
