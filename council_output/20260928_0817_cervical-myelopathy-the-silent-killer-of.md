## Introduction

Cervical myelopathy is the most common cause of non-traumatic spinal cord dysfunction in adults over 55 years of age. Incidence estimates range from roughly 4 to 41 cases per million population per year, while surgical incidence is frequently cited near 1.6 per 100 000. What makes cervical myelopathy especially dangerous is its quiet onset: subtle clumsiness, numbness, or gait change can progress for months or years before patients seek help. By the time grip strength collapses or fine finger movements disappear, irreversible damage to the corticospinal tracts and intrinsic hand pathways has often already occurred. In Pakistan, tertiary-centre series from Karachi, Lahore and Islamabad repeatedly document late presentation with advanced hand and gait deficits, reflecting limited early MRI access and delayed neurosurgical referral. This article explains why cervical myelopathy earns the label “silent killer of hand function,” how we recognise it, and how timely decompression can halt further loss.

## What is it?

Cervical myelopathy, particularly the degenerative form known as degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM) or cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM), is chronic compression and dysfunction of the cervical spinal cord. Degenerative changes—disc herniation, osteophytes, ligamentum flavum hypertrophy and facet arthropathy—narrow the spinal canal and repeatedly injure the cord with every neck movement. The result is progressive demyelination, axonal loss and ischaemia, especially affecting the lateral corticospinal tracts that control skilled hand movements and the dorsal columns that carry proprioception.  

Clinically this produces the classic “myelopathy hand”: loss of dexterity, difficulty with buttons or writing, intrinsic muscle wasting, and a characteristic clumsy grip. Unlike an acute traumatic cord injury, the process is insidious; many patients attribute early symptoms to “age” or “arthritis” until irreversible deficits appear. Natural-history studies show that the majority of patients with moderate or severe disease deteriorate over time and that spontaneous major recovery is uncommon. Early recognition therefore remains the single most important factor determining long-term hand function.

## Causes

The dominant cause is spondylotic degeneration of the cervical spine. Age-related disc desiccation leads to loss of height, annular bulging and osteophyte formation. Concurrent hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum and ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament further reduce canal diameter. Dynamic factors—extension that buckles the ligamentum flavum or flexion that stretches the cord over osteophytes—produce repetitive microtrauma.  

Less common contributors include congenital canal stenosis, rheumatoid pannus, tumours and, rarely, infectious or inflammatory lesions. In the Pakistani population, occupational neck strain, delayed presentation of cervical spondylosis, and limited routine screening amplify the clinical burden. Whatever the precise mix of static and dynamic compression, the final common pathway is chronic cord ischaemia and demyelination that preferentially damages the pathways serving the hands.

## Symptoms - bold red flags

Early cervical myelopathy is subtle; late disease is devastating. Watch for these **red-flag** features:

- **Progressive loss of fine motor control** — difficulty buttoning shirts, writing, or handling coins  
- **Intrinsic hand-muscle wasting** and the “myelopathy hand” posture  
- **Numbness or paraesthesia** in a non-dermatomal, often glove-like distribution  
- **Gait instability** or “heavy legs,” especially in the dark  
- **Hyperreflexia**, Hoffmann sign, inverted radial reflex, or clonus  
- **Lhermitte phenomenon** (electric shock on neck flexion)  
- **Bowel or bladder urgency** in more advanced cases  

Patients frequently report dropping objects or needing two hands for tasks previously done with one. Because pain may be minimal or absent, many delay seeking care until grip strength and dexterity are permanently impaired. Any combination of hand clumsiness plus upper-motor-neuron signs in the legs warrants urgent imaging and specialist review.

## Diagnosis at Faraz Hospital

At Faraz Hospital, Bahawalpur, evaluation begins with a detailed neurological examination focused on hand function (grip dynamometry, nine-hole peg test), gait, and long-tract signs. We use the modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association (mJOA) score and Nurick grade to quantify severity.  

Magnetic resonance imaging remains the gold-standard for visualising cord compression, T2 hyperintensity and canal stenosis. When MRI is unavailable or contraindicated, or when bony detail is required for surgical planning, we employ the BodyTom portable CT scanner to obtain high-resolution cervical imaging. Electrophysiological studies (SSEP, MEP) are added selectively to confirm cord dysfunction and exclude mimics such as motor-neuron disease or peripheral neuropathy. This multimodal approach follows international recommendations for accurate staging and timely decision-making.

## Treatment

Evidence-based guidelines from AOSpine and the AANS/CNS recommend surgical decompression for patients with moderate-to-severe cervical myelopathy (mJOA ≤12–14) to halt progression and improve function. Mild, non-progressive cases may be observed with structured follow-up, but the natural history shows that most untreated patients deteriorate.  

At Faraz Hospital we perform both anterior (ACDF, corpectomy) and posterior (laminoplasty, laminectomy with fusion) approaches, selecting the strategy according to sagittal alignment, number of stenotic levels and patient factors. Surgery is performed under high-magnification visualisation with the Zeiss operating microscope, enabling precise decompression while protecting residual cord tissue. Intra-operative BodyTom CT confirms adequacy of decompression and hardware placement when needed.  

Randomised and prospective data summarised in the AOSpine guidelines demonstrate that surgery improves mJOA scores, reduces pain and stabilises or restores hand function in the majority of appropriately selected patients. Conservative care (collar, physiotherapy, activity modification) is reserved strictly for mild, stable disease; it does not reverse established cord damage. Early referral therefore remains critical—especially in resource-constrained settings where late presentation is common.

## Recovery

Post-operative recovery of hand function depends on pre-operative severity and duration of symptoms. Patients with shorter symptom duration and higher baseline mJOA scores regain dexterity more reliably. Most notice reduced numbness and improved gait within weeks; intrinsic muscle strength and fine motor control continue to improve for 6–12 months.  

A structured rehabilitation programme—occupational therapy for hand dexterity, gait training and isometric neck exercises—maximises gains. We counsel patients that surgery primarily stops further loss; dramatic recovery of long-standing wasting is less predictable. Regular follow-up with repeat mJOA scoring and, when indicated, imaging ensures that any residual or adjacent-segment issues are addressed promptly. In our experience, patients who reach the operating theatre before severe, irreversible hand dysfunction have the best chance of returning to independent daily activities.

## FAQ

**1. Is cervical myelopathy the same as a pinched nerve?**  
No. Radiculopathy affects a single nerve root and usually causes arm pain; myelopathy compresses the spinal cord itself and produces bilateral hand clumsiness, hyperreflexia and gait change.

**2. Can physiotherapy cure cervical myelopathy?**  
Physiotherapy may help mild, stable cases or post-operative recovery, but it cannot relieve mechanical cord compression. Moderate-to-severe disease requires surgical decompression.

**3. How quickly does hand function deteriorate?**  
Progression is variable—some patients remain stable for years, others decline over months. Once moderate deficits appear, further loss is likely without treatment.

**4. Is surgery safe in older adults?**  
Modern microsurgical techniques and intra-operative imaging have markedly reduced morbidity. Age alone is not a contraindication; physiological status and severity of myelopathy guide decision-making.

**5. What happens if I ignore the symptoms?**  
Untreated moderate-to-severe cervical myelopathy carries a high risk of permanent loss of hand dexterity, walking ability and independence. Early intervention offers the best chance of preserving function.
