# Intraventricular tumors and hydrocephalus

Intraventricular masses sit directly in the pathways that move cerebrospinal fluid. When a colloid cyst, central neurocytoma, or intraventricular ependymoma blocks the foramen of Monro, aqueduct, or fourth-ventricle outlets, pressure can rise within hours. That acute CSF obstruction risk is why timely surgery is not elective cosmetics—it can be life-saving.

## What changed / Why it matters
Modern practice still rests on three linked priorities: restore CSF flow, obtain tissue, and remove as much tumor as is safely possible without injuring deep veins or eloquent walls.

**Colloid cyst** (typically third ventricle at the foramen of Monro) remains the classic intermittent or sudden blocker. Even small lesions can produce acute hydrocephalus, herniation, or sudden deterioration. Surgical goals are definitive cyst removal and permanent reopening of both foramina. Endoscopic resection or microsurgical approaches (transcallosal or transcortical) are selected by size, attachment, and surgeon experience; the non-negotiable endpoint is a clear CSF pathway, with external drainage only as a bridge when the patient arrives in crisis.

**Central neurocytoma** usually arises in the lateral ventricle near the septum pellucidum and can obstruct one or both foramina. It is typically a WHO grade 2 neuronal tumor. Priority is maximal safe resection for diagnosis, cytoreduction, and hydrocephalus control. Incomplete removal or residual attachment near the thalamostriate veins may leave patients shunt-dependent; planned second-look or adjuvant options are individualized after histology and residual volume are known.

**Intraventricular ependymoma** (lateral or fourth ventricle in adults and children) combines mass effect with a high chance of trapping CSF compartments. Surgery aims for safe gross-total resection when anatomy allows, because residual disease drives recurrence risk and often needs radiotherapy. Fourth-ventricle cases demand meticulous protection of the floor nuclei; lateral-ventricle cases demand respect for the internal capsule and deep venous system. Perioperative external ventricular drainage, endoscopic third ventriculostomy in selected upstream blocks, or later shunting may still be required if pathways do not reopen.

Across all three, imaging triage (CT for emergency hydrocephalus; MRI with contrast and CSF-flow sequences for planning), early transfer to a center that can operate the same day if needed, and avoidance of lumbar puncture in obstructive hydrocephalus remain foundational. Delaying definitive pathway restoration while “watching” a symptomatic block exposes the patient to avoidable secondary brain injury.

## Patient takeaway
Headache that worsens when lying flat, vomiting, visual blurring, sudden drowsiness, or a known ventricular lesion with enlarging ventricles on scan should prompt urgent neurosurgical review. Not every intraventricular spot needs immediate surgery, but any sign of CSF obstruction does. Ask your team which approach they recommend, how they will protect memory and motor pathways, and whether temporary drainage or a shunt may still be needed after tumor removal.
