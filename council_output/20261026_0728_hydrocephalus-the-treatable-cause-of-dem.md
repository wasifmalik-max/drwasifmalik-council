## Introduction

Most families facing progressive memory loss, unsteady walking and urinary problems in an older relative assume irreversible Alzheimer’s disease or vascular dementia. Yet one under-recognised condition—idiopathic normal-pressure hydrocephalus (iNPH)—accounts for an estimated 2–6 % of dementia cases and is potentially reversible. Population studies place prevalence between roughly 0.2 % and 2.9 % in people over 65, rising with age. When cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is diverted by a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt, gait improves in 70–90 % of carefully selected patients and cognition can partially recover. In Pakistan, congenital and post-infectious hydrocephalus dominate paediatric practice, while adult iNPH remains sparsely diagnosed because of limited MRI access and low awareness. Early recognition at a neurosurgical centre can restore independence that would otherwise be lost. This article explains the science, red-flag symptoms, evidence-based work-up and modern VP-shunt surgery so patients and families can seek timely specialist care.

## What is it?

Hydrocephalus is an abnormal accumulation of cerebrospinal fluid within the brain’s ventricular system, producing ventriculomegaly. In the classic high-pressure forms seen after haemorrhage, infection or congenital aqueduct stenosis, intracranial pressure rises and life-threatening herniation can occur. Idiopathic normal-pressure hydrocephalus, by contrast, presents in older adults with enlarged ventricles yet measured CSF pressure that remains within the normal range. The exact pathophysiology is still debated; impaired CSF absorption at the arachnoid granulations, reduced intracranial compliance and altered pulsatile flow are leading hypotheses. The result is a slowly progressive triad of gait disturbance, urinary incontinence and cognitive decline—often mislabelled simply as “old age” or degenerative dementia. Unlike most neurodegenerative dementias, the mechanical component of ventricular enlargement is amenable to surgical CSF diversion. A properly functioning hydrocephalus VP shunt can therefore reverse or stabilise disability that would otherwise progress relentlessly. Distinguishing iNPH from Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease or multi-infarct dementia requires systematic clinical and imaging evaluation rather than assumption.

## Causes

Hydrocephalus is classified as congenital or acquired, and as obstructive (non-communicating) or communicating. Congenital causes common in Pakistan include neural-tube defects, aqueduct stenosis and post-infectious scarring after neonatal meningitis. Acquired aetiologies encompass subarachnoid or intraventricular haemorrhage, trauma, tumours that block CSF pathways, and chronic meningitis. Idiopathic normal-pressure hydrocephalus has no single identifiable cause; age-related changes in CSF dynamics and cerebrovascular disease are associated factors. Secondary normal-pressure hydrocephalus can follow remote subarachnoid haemorrhage or meningitis. In every case the final common pathway is imbalance between CSF production and absorption or obstruction to flow, leading to progressive ventricular enlargement and periventricular tissue stress. Identifying an obstructive lesion is critical because endoscopic third ventriculostomy may be preferable to a VP shunt. When imaging shows communicating hydrocephalus and the clinical triad is present, iNPH becomes the working diagnosis and shunt candidacy is explored.

## Symptoms – bold red flags

The classic Hakim triad remains the clinical cornerstone:

- **Magnetic gait** – slow, shuffling, wide-based walking as if the feet are stuck to the floor; frequent falls; difficulty turning.  
- **Urinary urgency or incontinence** – initially frequency, later frank incontinence without sensory loss.  
- **Cognitive decline** – subcortical pattern: slowed thinking, impaired executive function, apathy, reduced attention; memory loss is usually milder than in Alzheimer’s disease.

**Additional red flags** that should prompt urgent neurosurgical referral:
- Rapid progression of gait failure over months rather than years.  
- Ventriculomegaly on CT or MRI out of proportion to cortical atrophy.  
- Temporary improvement after large-volume lumbar puncture (CSF tap test).  
- New or worsening headaches, visual obscurations or papilloedema (suggesting higher-pressure hydrocephalus).  
- Any focal neurological deficit or signs of raised intracranial pressure in a younger patient.

Because gait often responds best to shunting, early isolation of the walking disturbance is especially valuable. Families should not dismiss the combination of “walking like a magnet, wetting, and slowing mind” as inevitable ageing.

## Diagnosis at Faraz Hospital

At Faraz Hospital, Bahawalpur, suspected hydrocephalus is evaluated with a structured protocol. Detailed history and neurological examination document the triad and exclude mimics. High-resolution CT or MRI (when available) confirms ventriculomegaly, assesses for obstruction, and looks for supporting features such as disproportionately enlarged subarachnoid-space hydrocephalus (DESH). We exclude mass lesions, acute haemorrhage and active infection. A CSF tap test or continuous lumbar drainage is performed in selected patients to predict shunt responsiveness: objective gait timing and cognitive scores are recorded before and after removal of 30–50 mL of CSF. Opening pressure is measured. Multidisciplinary discussion weighs comorbidities, surgical risk and expected benefit. When imaging or clinical status is urgent, intraoperative mobile CT (BodyTom) can refine trajectory planning. This systematic approach follows international guidance that emphasises careful candidate selection before offering a hydrocephalus VP shunt.

## Treatment

The definitive treatment for symptomatic iNPH that responds to temporary CSF drainage is permanent CSF diversion, most commonly a ventriculoperitoneal shunt. Evidence-based guidelines support this pathway. Relkin et al. (Neurosurgery 2005; [PMID removed - unverified]) codified clinical diagnostic criteria. Marmarou et al. (Neurosurgery 2005; [PMID removed - unverified]) reviewed surgical management and reported meaningful improvement, especially in gait, when patients are properly selected. An AAN-linked evidence review ([PMID removed - unverified]) further contextualises evaluation and treatment decisions. Contemporary programmable valves allow non-invasive pressure adjustment, reducing over- and under-drainage. At Faraz Hospital the procedure is performed under microscopic visualisation with a Zeiss operating microscope for precise ventricular catheter placement; BodyTom intraoperative CT verifies catheter position and excludes immediate haemorrhage before wound closure. Antibiotic-impregnated catheters and strict asepsis minimise infection risk—a critical consideration in our setting. Endoscopic third ventriculostomy is reserved for obstructive cases. Risks (infection, subdural haematoma, valve malfunction, over-drainage) are discussed openly; benefits are greatest for gait, moderate for continence and more variable for cognition. Shared decision-making with the patient and family is mandatory.

## Recovery

Most patients notice gait improvement within days to weeks after a successful hydrocephalus VP shunt; some walk more steadily even before discharge. Continence may recover over weeks to months. Cognitive gains, when they occur, appear more gradually and are often incomplete. Early mobilisation, physiotherapy and fall-prevention strategies accelerate functional recovery. Patients are taught to recognise signs of under-drainage (recurrent symptoms) or over-drainage (postural headache, subdural collections). Programmable valves can be re-adjusted in clinic. Follow-up imaging and clinical scoring at 1, 3 and 12 months guide long-term management. In resource-constrained environments, reliable follow-up and prompt treatment of shunt infection or blockage remain essential for sustained benefit. Many individuals regain sufficient independence to reduce caregiver burden substantially.

## FAQ

**1. Is every case of hydrocephalus treated with a VP shunt?**  
No. Obstructive hydrocephalus may be treated by endoscopic third ventriculostomy. Asymptomatic ventriculomegaly does not require surgery. Only patients with probable iNPH who improve after a CSF drainage trial are strong shunt candidates.

**2. How successful is a hydrocephalus VP shunt for dementia symptoms?**  
Gait improves in approximately 70–90 % of well-selected patients. Cognitive improvement is more modest and variable; early intervention yields better results.

**3. What are the main risks of VP shunt surgery?**  
Infection, haemorrhage, over- or under-drainage, seizure and the need for revision. Modern valves, antibiotic catheters and intraoperative imaging reduce but do not eliminate these risks.

**4. Can iNPH be diagnosed without MRI?**  
CT can demonstrate ventriculomegaly and guide initial decisions. MRI provides superior soft-tissue detail and is preferred when available, but lack of MRI should not delay specialist assessment.

**5. Is the condition common in Pakistan?**  
Paediatric post-infectious and congenital hydrocephalus are frequent. Adult iNPH is under-diagnosed; greater awareness and access to neurosurgical evaluation are needed.

## Conclusion + CTA

Idiopathic normal-pressure hydrocephalus is one of the few truly treatable causes of dementia-like decline. Recognition of the gait–continence–cognition triad, confirmatory imaging and a predictive CSF tap test can identify patients who stand to regain mobility and quality of life after a hydrocephalus VP shunt. Delayed diagnosis condemns individuals to progressive, avoidable disability. If you or a family member show these warning signs, seek specialist neurosurgical assessment without delay.
