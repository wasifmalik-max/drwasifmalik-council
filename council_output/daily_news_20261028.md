# Elderly GBM: Surgery vs Biopsy-First

In older adults with glioblastoma (GBM), the reflex to “do everything” can harm more than help. Frailty, comorbidity, and limited physiologic reserve often matter as much as the MRI. Contemporary practice increasingly pairs **selective resection** with **hypofractionated radiotherapy pathways**, reserving maximal surgery for patients who can absorb the recovery cost—and biopsy-first strategies when they cannot.

## What changed / Why it matters

GBM in the elderly is not simply “young GBM with a higher birthday.” Median survival is shorter, treatment toxicity is less forgiving, and postoperative decline can erase any oncologic gain from a larger resection. Multidisciplinary guidance and trial-informed pathways have therefore shifted selection away from age alone toward **frailty-adapted decisions**.

**Surgery versus biopsy.** When a lesion is accessible, the patient has acceptable performance status, and resection can safely reduce mass effect or clarify diagnosis without catastrophic neurologic cost, cytoreductive surgery remains reasonable. Extent of resection still correlates with outcome in fit older adults, but the margin for error is thin: new deficits, prolonged hospitalization, and delayed adjuvant therapy often outweigh millimetres of residual tumor. In patients with marked frailty, significant medical comorbidity, deep or eloquent disease where safe debulking is unlikely, or clear preference against aggressive recovery, **stereotactic or open biopsy** establishes histology and molecular markers (including MGMT methylation where available) with lower physiologic burden—then moves the patient promptly to radiotherapy and systemic options.

**Hypofractionation as the backbone adjuvant path.** For many adults roughly ≥65–70 years, or younger patients who are frail, shortened radiotherapy courses (commonly on the order of 40 Gy in 15 fractions, with other abbreviated schedules used by indication) are preferred over six-week normofractionation. Randomized and cooperative-group evidence has supported hypofractionation with or without temozolomide, tailored to fitness and MGMT context, reducing time on treatment and cumulative fatigue while preserving meaningful disease control relative to longer courses in this population. Concurrent and adjuvant temozolomide is individualized: more often favored when MGMT is methylated and marrow/organ reserve allows; omitted or abbreviated when toxicity risk dominates.

**Why this matters at the bedside.** The goal is not maximal therapy on paper—it is **completed, tolerable therapy** that matches reserve. Avoiding overtreatment means skipping heroic resection in patients who will not reach adjuvant care in usable shape, and skipping protracted chemoradiation when a shorter path is evidence-aligned and kinder. Early palliative-care concurrent involvement, steroid stewardship, seizure control, and honest discussion of goals (independence, time at home, symptom relief) are part of the same decision, not an afterthought.

## Patient takeaway

If you or a family member is older and newly found to have a likely GBM, ask three practical questions: (1) Is the aim safe tumor reduction or diagnosis with minimal recovery cost? (2) What does frailty and day-to-day function suggest about surviving surgery and finishing radiation? (3) Is a **shorter radiotherapy plan**, with chemotherapy only if benefit is likely, the better fit? A biopsy-first or limited-resection path plus hypofractionation is often appropriate care—not “giving up.”
