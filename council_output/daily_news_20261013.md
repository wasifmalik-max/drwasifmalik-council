# Vestibular schwannoma: wait-scan vs intervene

Incidental cerebellopontine angle (CPA) lesions—most often vestibular schwannomas—are increasingly found on MRI obtained for unrelated symptoms. For patients and referring clinicians, the immediate question is practical: observe with serial imaging, offer microsurgical resection, or refer for stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS)? Counseling must balance tumor control against hearing preservation and facial-nerve function, using the lesion’s size, growth pattern, hearing class, and patient age rather than a one-size-fits-all rule.

## What changed / Why it matters
Contemporary practice has shifted from routine early intervention toward risk-stratified management. Small, stable, intracanalicular or minimally extracanalicular tumors in patients with serviceable hearing are frequently managed with wait-and-scan protocols (typically MRI at 6 months, then annually if stable). Documented growth, progressive hearing loss, or brainstem compression triggers active treatment. Microsurgery remains definitive for larger tumors or those causing mass effect; approach selection (retrosigmoid, middle fossa, or translabyrinthine) is driven by hearing status and tumor extent. Hearing-preservation attempts are realistic mainly for smaller lesions with good preoperative pure-tone and speech scores; facial-nerve anatomic preservation rates are high in experienced hands, yet transient or permanent House–Brackmann decline still occurs and must be discussed preoperatively. SRS (single-session or hypofractionated) offers high rates of long-term tumor control for small- to medium-sized schwannomas with low immediate morbidity; delayed hearing decline can still appear over years, and facial neuropathy risk is generally low but not zero. No single modality is universally superior: observation avoids upfront cranial-nerve injury but accepts possible later growth and hearing loss; surgery maximizes immediate decompression and tissue diagnosis at the cost of operative risk; SRS minimizes hospitalization while committing the patient to lifelong imaging surveillance and possible salvage treatment. Multidisciplinary review (neurosurgery, neurotology, radiation oncology) and shared decision-making therefore constitute the current standard for incidental CPA lesions.

## Patient takeaway
If an MRI shows a small vestibular schwannoma and hearing remains useful, a structured observation plan with scheduled scans is often appropriate and does not close the door to later surgery or SRS. Growth, new imbalance, facial weakness, or declining hearing should prompt timely re-evaluation. Treatment choice is individualized—size, growth rate, hearing class, age, medical fitness, and personal preference all matter. Ask specifically about expected rates of hearing preservation, facial-nerve function, and the need for lifelong follow-up imaging after any chosen path.
