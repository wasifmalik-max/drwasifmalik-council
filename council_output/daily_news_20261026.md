# Metastatic Spine vs Intracranial MDT Sequencing

Concurrent brain and spine metastases create competing demands on operating-room time, stereotactic radiosurgery slots, and fractionated radiotherapy schedules. Without deliberate multidisciplinary sequencing, an urgent spinal decompression can postpone intracranial radiosurgery, or an early craniotomy can delay cord-stabilizing radiation—both pathways risk neurologic decline and interrupted systemic therapy.

## What changed / Why it matters
Modern neuro-oncology practice increasingly treats brain and spine metastases as a single continuum rather than sequential silos. Multidisciplinary team (MDT) meetings now routinely map lesion acuity, ambulatory status, and radiosensitivity in one sitting. Intracranial lesions that threaten herniation or seizures typically claim first operative priority when mass effect is progressive; conversely, unstable spinal metastases with cord compression or mechanical instability often supersede elective intracranial procedures because irreversible paraplegia carries higher immediate morbidity. Once surgical windows are ranked, radiotherapy planning follows the same hierarchy: stereotactic body radiotherapy or conventional fractionated courses for the spine are scheduled so that they do not collide with postoperative intracranial cavity irradiation or whole-brain fields. Shared electronic booking boards and same-week joint clinics reduce the classic “OR-then-wait-for-RT” lag that historically stretched beyond two weeks. The practical gain is fewer cancelled lists, earlier start of systemic agents, and clearer documentation of why one site was treated before the other—information that later auditors and referring oncologists require. For the operating neurosurgeon this framework prevents last-minute priority conflicts and protects the radiotherapy timeline that underpins local control.

## Patient takeaway
If imaging shows deposits in both brain and spine, ask the team which site will be addressed first and why. A coordinated plan usually means surgery or radiation for the most threatening lesion within days, followed promptly by treatment of the second site, rather than open-ended waiting lists. Bring an up-to-date medication list and recent scans to every MDT discussion so sequencing decisions remain accurate.
