# Brain metastases: surgery vs SRS selection

In oligometastatic brain disease, the MDT choice between microsurgical resection and stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is among the most frequent decisions that immediately shape neurologic function, steroid dependence, and time to systemic therapy. Selection is not “surgery versus radiation” in the abstract; it is matched to lesion size, mass effect, location, histology, performance status, and the need for tissue.

## What changed / Why it matters

Contemporary guideline-aligned practice (AANS/CNS, ASTRO, and EANO frameworks for limited brain metastases) treats carefully staged oligometastatic disease—commonly one to a few discrete lesions with controlled or controllable extracranial disease—as a setting where local control can be achieved with either modality when indications are respected. Resection is favored when there is significant mass effect or raised intracranial pressure, obstructive hydrocephalus risk, progressive neurologic deficit from a surgically accessible lesion, cystic or hemorrhagic morphology unlikely to respond promptly to radiosurgery alone, diagnostic uncertainty (no secure extracranial primary sampling), or radioresistant biology where debulking plus cavity-directed adjuvant radiosurgery is planned. SRS is preferred for smaller, well-circumscribed metastases without clinically meaningful mass effect, deep or eloquent sites where resection risk outweighs benefit, multiple small targets treatable in one or few sessions, and patients who need minimal interruption of systemic treatment. Postoperative cavity SRS has largely replaced routine whole-brain radiotherapy after complete resection of limited disease in appropriate candidates, reducing neurocognitive burden while preserving local control. Conversely, upfront SRS without surgery is inappropriate when urgent decompression is required or when imaging cannot reliably exclude abscess, primary brain tumor, or treatment-related necrosis. MDT quality hinges on thin-slice MRI review, realistic assessment of performance status and expected systemic options, and honest discussion of risks: surgical morbidity and recovery time versus radiation necrosis, steroid exposure, and delayed local failure. For the practicing neurosurgeon, the “immediate QoL” pivot is often whether the patient leaves the hospital walking, speaking, and off high-dose dexamethasone sooner—or whether a non-operative path avoids an operation the patient did not need.

## Patient takeaway

If you have a small number of brain metastases, ask whether each lesion is causing pressure symptoms, whether tissue is needed, and whether SRS or surgery better protects function while your cancer team continues systemic care.
