## Introduction  
A sudden, explosive headache that reaches maximum intensity within seconds is classically called “the worst headache of your life.” In neurosurgery this phrase is a red-flag warning for aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH). Unruptured intracranial aneurysms affect roughly 3 % of adults. When one ruptures, blood floods the subarachnoid space at arterial pressure. Global incidence of aSAH is 6–9 per 100 000 person-years; case fatality still ranges 35–50 %, and one-third of survivors are left with major disability. In Pakistani tertiary centres the same pattern is seen—often in younger adults with heavy smoking or uncontrolled hypertension—compounded by delayed presentation and limited round-the-clock endovascular access. Early recognition and securement of the aneurysm remain the only proven ways to reduce re-bleeding and improve outcome. This article summarises current evidence so patients and families understand why every minute counts.

## What is a Brain Aneurysm?  
A brain aneurysm is a focal, balloon-like dilatation of a cerebral artery wall, most often at branching points of the circle of Willis. The commonest morphological type is the saccular (berry) aneurysm; fusiform and dissecting forms are less frequent. The wall of the sac is deficient in the normal muscular and elastic layers, leaving only a thin adventitia and intima that can rupture under hemodynamic stress. Rupture produces aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage—blood under high pressure in the cerebrospinal-fluid spaces surrounding the brain. Unruptured aneurysms may remain silent for years or produce mass-effect symptoms (cranial-nerve palsy, seizure). Once rupture occurs, the immediate threats are raised intracranial pressure, acute hydrocephalus, and a second bleed, which is most likely in the first 24 hours. Understanding this pathophysiology explains why “thunderclap” headache plus any neurological change demands emergency imaging rather than simple analgesia.

## Causes  
The precise trigger for aneurysm formation is multifactorial. Chronic hypertension and cigarette smoking are the strongest modifiable risks; both damage the arterial wall and accelerate growth. Genetic predisposition is clear in first-degree relatives and in connective-tissue disorders such as autosomal-dominant polycystic kidney disease. Female sex, age over 50, heavy alcohol use, and prior aneurysmal SAH further elevate risk. In South-Asian series, including those from Karachi and Lahore, smoking and poorly controlled hypertension dominate the comorbidity profile, and aneurysms frequently present a decade earlier than in Western cohorts. Rarely, mycotic (infectious) or traumatic aneurysms occur. No single factor guarantees rupture; rather, size (>7 mm), irregular shape, and location (posterior circulation) interact with systemic blood-pressure spikes to determine the moment of hemorrhage.

## Symptoms – Bold Red Flags  
**Thunderclap headache**—sudden onset, maximal within seconds to a minute, often described as “the worst headache of my life”—is the hallmark of aSAH.  
**Neck stiffness or pain** appears within hours from meningeal irritation.  
**Vomiting**, photophobia, or brief loss of consciousness commonly accompany the pain.  
**Seizure** at onset occurs in up to 20 % of cases.  
**Focal neurological deficit** (third-nerve palsy, hemiparesis, dysphasia) may indicate enlarging hematoma or early ischemia.  
**Altered mental status** ranging from confusion to coma signals raised intracranial pressure or hydrocephalus.  
Any combination of these features after a sudden severe headache is a neurosurgical emergency. Milder “sentinel” headaches days earlier can represent a minor leak; dismissing them as migraine risks catastrophic re-bleed. Patients in Pakistan frequently reach hospital late because the first episode is attributed to heat, stress, or “gas.” Immediate transfer to a centre with CT and neurosurgical capability is mandatory.

## Diagnosis at Faraz Hospital  
At Faraz Hospital, Bahawalpur, every patient with suspected thunderclap headache undergoes non-contrast CT of the brain within minutes of arrival. Modern multi-slice CT detects >95 % of aSAH if performed in the first 24 hours. When CT is negative yet clinical suspicion remains high, lumbar puncture looking for xanthochromia is performed after six hours. Once SAH is confirmed, CT angiography (CTA) delineates aneurysm location, size, and morphology. Digital-subtraction angiography remains the gold standard when endovascular or complex surgical planning is required. Our workflow prioritises rapid imaging, blood-pressure control, and early neurosurgical review so that securement can occur before the peak re-bleed window. Families receive clear explanations at each step; no imaging report is ever issued without direct consultant oversight.

## Treatment  
Evidence-based care follows AHA/ASA and European Stroke Organization guidelines. The ruptured aneurysm must be secured as early as feasible—preferably within 24–72 hours—by either microsurgical clipping or endovascular coiling. Choice depends on aneurysm anatomy, patient age, and local expertise. Nimodipine 60 mg orally every four hours for 21 days reduces delayed cerebral ischemia. Strict blood-pressure management, seizure prophylaxis when indicated, and continuous ICU monitoring for hydrocephalus or vasospasm are mandatory.  

At Faraz Hospital we utilise the portable BodyTom CT for immediate post-operative and ICU scanning without hazardous transfer, and the Zeiss operating microscope for high-magnification microsurgical clipping under continuous neuro-monitoring. These technologies, combined with a dedicated neurosurgical ICU pathway, mirror international standards even in a resource-variable setting. Re-bleed risk falls dramatically once the aneurysm is excluded from the circulation; thereafter attention shifts to prevention of secondary brain injury. Multidisciplinary input—neurosurgery, critical care, physiotherapy—begins on day one.

## Recovery  
Survivors of aSAH face a prolonged recovery. Hospital stay averages 14–21 days; many require temporary external ventricular drainage. Cognitive fatigue, mood change, and subtle executive dysfunction are common even when motor recovery is good. Structured neuro-rehabilitation, blood-pressure optimisation, smoking cessation, and screening of first-degree relatives for unruptured aneurysms form the long-term plan. In Pakistani series, outcomes improve markedly when patients reach a neurosurgical centre within 24 hours and receive protocolised care; delays in transfer or ICU bed availability remain the greatest local obstacles. Regular outpatient review with the operating neurosurgeon ensures hydrocephalus, seizures, or residual aneurysm remnants are detected early.

## FAQ  
**1. Is every sudden headache a brain aneurysm?**  
No. Migraine, tension headache, and sinus disease are far more common. However, a truly thunderclap onset plus neck stiffness or neurological change must be treated as SAH until proven otherwise.  

**2. Can an unruptured aneurysm be watched?**  
Small, asymptomatic aneurysms <7 mm in the anterior circulation may be observed with serial imaging after risk–benefit discussion. Larger or irregular lesions often warrant elective treatment.  

**3. What is the difference between clipping and coiling?**  
Clipping is open microsurgery that places a titanium clip across the aneurysm neck. Coiling is endovascular placement of platinum coils inside the sac. Both are effective; anatomy and patient factors decide.  

**4. How soon must treatment start after rupture?**  
Re-bleed risk is highest in the first 24 hours. Guidelines recommend securement as early as logistically possible, ideally within 72 hours.  

**5. Should my family be screened?**  
First-degree relatives of patients with aSAH or known aneurysms have elevated risk and may benefit from non-invasive CTA or MRA screening, especially if two or more family members are affected.
